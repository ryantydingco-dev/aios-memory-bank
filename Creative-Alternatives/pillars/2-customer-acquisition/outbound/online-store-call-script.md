# Online-Store Cold-Call Script

> Use for camps, racquet clubs, schools, teams, and other organizations with a
> recurring audience that wants branded gear. The goal is permission to send a custom
> store preview—not to close or quote on the first call.

## The pitch in one sentence

> “We build and run the branded store; your families or members order directly, we
> handle the products, production, individual shipping, and support, and your
> organization receives a share of sales.”

The store has no upfront inventory purchase for the organization. Maclaine confirms
product prices, the organization's exact share, and any commercial terms.

## Live script

### Opener

> “Hi [First], Ryan Tydingco with Creative Alternatives. I know I'm calling you out of
> the blue. Can I take 25 seconds to explain why I picked [Organization], and then you
> can tell me if it's irrelevant?”

### Reason and store pitch

> “I saw [specific season, event, registration period, fundraiser, or member trigger].
> We've been family-run since 1999 and have spent decades producing gear for
> [camps/clubs/schools]. We now build branded online stores where your families or
> members order directly. We handle the designs, production, individual shipping, and
> customer support, so your team doesn't collect sizes, hold inventory, or pack boxes.
> The organization also receives a share of sales.”

Then ask:

> “How are people buying [Organization] gear today?”

Stop talking and listen.

### Useful follow-up questions

Ask no more than two before recommending the next step:

- “What works well with the current setup?”
- “Who handles the designs, orders, and support?”
- “Do people order individually, or does the organization buy in bulk?”
- “When does demand peak?”
- “Is the goal access to good gear, fundraising, or both?”
- “Who would need to approve the logo and store?”

### Close for the preview

> “The easiest way to judge this isn't another sales pitch. I can put your logo and
> colors on a small store preview with a few relevant products. There is no commitment.
> If it looks useful, we discuss the details; if not, you can tell me no. Worth seeing?”

If yes:

> “Great. What's the best email, is [logo source] the current logo, and would your
> audience respond better to [relevant option A] or [option B]?”

Confirm exactly when the preview will arrive and when Ryan will follow up. Do not promise
timing that the art/fulfillment queue cannot support.

## Lane-specific reason lines

**Camp**

> “I saw [registration/event/session trigger]. Families still want the sweatshirt after
> camp ends, but most camps don't want leftover stock or another fulfillment job.”

**Squash or racquet club**

> “I saw [league/tournament/member trigger]. Members and teams want club gear, but it
> shouldn't require the front desk to become a shipping department.”

**School/PTA/booster**

> “I saw [season/fundraiser/back-to-school trigger]. Spirit wear usually turns into
> volunteers collecting sizes, money, and boxes. The store removes that administration.”

**Youth sports/team**

> “I saw [registration/season/tournament trigger]. Parents order directly, so coaches
> and team managers don't have to guess sizes or hold inventory.”

## Online-store objections

### “We already have a store.”

> “Good—you've already validated that people want access to gear. Is the store producing
> the participation you want, and who currently owns new designs, promotion, returns,
> and customer support?”

If everything works well, do not manufacture a problem. Ask when the arrangement renews
or exit graciously.

### “We already have a vendor/local printer.”

> “That makes sense, and I'm not asking you to replace a partner who performs. Do they
> also run the individual online orders, shipping, support, and ongoing product updates,
> or does some of that stay with your team?”

### “We use Printful/another print-on-demand platform.”

> “Understood. Are you happy with the product quality, design help, customer experience,
> and what the organization receives? If all four work, I won't invent a reason to
> switch.”

### “We don't have a budget.”

> “Understood. The organization doesn't pre-buy the store inventory; individuals order
> directly. Is the concern really budget, or not wanting another program to manage?”

### “What's the catch?”

> “Creative Alternatives earns its margin when an item is produced, and the organization
> receives a share. The organization isn't purchasing inventory upfront. Maclaine
> confirms the exact economics before anything moves forward.”

### “How much do we make?”

> “It depends on the approved products and store structure. Maclaine will give you the
> exact number—I don't want to invent a payout on the phone.”

### “What will products cost our families/members?”

> “That depends on the product mix, decoration, and approved store structure. We can
> build the preview first, then Maclaine gives you the exact retail pricing before you
> approve anything.”

### “Our people won't buy.”

> “That may be right. Have you tried before? If so, was the issue the products, design,
> price, or that the store was never really launched? I wouldn't recommend repeating a
> setup that already failed.”

### “We tried a store and it failed.”

> “That's useful—not something to hide. What failed: product selection, designs, price,
> promotion, or fulfillment? If we can't point to a different approach, we shouldn't
> rebuild it.”

### “We don't have time to manage this.”

> “That's the reason for the model. CA handles design, production, individual shipping,
> and customer support. Your side approves the brand and shares the link. Is even that
> approval and launch step too much right now?”

### “We don't want to promote another thing.”

> “Fair. A store needs at least one real launch through a channel you already control.
> If the organization doesn't want to share it once, we shouldn't build it.”

### “We can't share our email list.”

> “You don't give us the list. Your team shares the store link through its own email,
> registration, social, or member channels.”

### “Parents/volunteers already handle this.”

> “Got it. Are they happy collecting sizes, money, boxes, and service questions? If they
> are, leave it alone. If that administration is the pain, that's what CA removes.”

### “We only sell gear once a year.”

> “That can still fit. When do you choose designs and vendors—not when do you deliver?
> That's the date I should work backward from.”

### “We're too small.”

> “Possibly. Roughly how many likely buyers are there, and is the demand recurring? If
> the economics don't fit, we'll say so rather than force it.”

### “Who handles shipping, returns, and customer questions?”

> “Creative Alternatives handles production, individual shipping, and store customer
> support. What's the specific failure scenario you're most concerned about?”

### “We're worried about quality.”

> “You should be—the product carries your brand. Is your concern the garment/product,
> the decoration, or consistency? We can use the preview and an approved sample/proof
> before launch.”

### “We need board/district/association approval.”

> “Makes sense. What does the approval group need to see: the visual, store process,
> economics, vendor documentation, or brand controls? I'll prepare the smallest useful
> package for that step.”

### “Fundraising or revenue sharing isn't allowed.”

> “Thanks for flagging it. Is the restriction on receiving proceeds, using outside
> vendors, or selling directly to families? We can only proceed within the policy.”

### “We can't use our logo without approval.”

> “Understood. Who controls brand approval, and what rules should the preview follow?
> We won't publish or launch anything without authorization.”

### “Can you launch it by [date]?”

> “Let me capture the exact date, audience, products, and approval process. Maclaine will
> verify feasibility before we promise a launch.”

### “Just send information.”

> “Happy to. A generic brochure won't tell you much—would it be more useful to send the
> store process or a visual using your actual brand?”

### “Not interested.”

> “Totally fair. Is that because branded gear isn't a priority, or because you already
> have the store and fulfillment covered?”

If they repeat the no, thank them and end the call.

### “Don't call again.”

> “Absolutely. I'll mark that now. Sorry for the interruption.”

Suppress immediately. No recovery attempt.

## Gatekeeper

> “I'm hoping you can point me in the right direction. Who owns the online spirit-wear
> or member-gear program? I noticed [trigger] and have one specific store idea—not a
> general catalog pitch.”

## Voicemail

> idea for a branded store where [families/members] order directly and your team doesn't
> manage inventory or shipping. I'll send a short note so you can decide if it's
> relevant. I'm at [number].”

## Store fit versus bulk fit

Use the store when there is a recurring audience placing individual orders. Switch to
the bulk motion when the buyer needs one quantity delivered to one place for a specific
event, staff group, team, or gifting program. Do not force every opportunity into the
store offer.

## Qualified yes checklist

Before ending a positive call, capture:

- verified email and current logo source;
- audience and approximate likely buyer count;
- primary goal: access, fundraising, or both;
- best initial product category;
- decision-maker and brand approver;
- launch/buying window;
- promised preview date and follow-up date.
